Mexico City's airport lies about 13 km from the city centre and handles around 44.6 million passengers a year (2025), making it one of the busiest in Latin America. It has **two terminals**, 2–3 km apart, linked by a free monorail shuttle (**Aerotrén**) — but one thing matters more than that: **Mexico City now has two airports**, so whenever you see "Mexico City" on a booking, check the three-letter code.

## The two terminals

| Code | Name | Main carriers | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T1 | Terminal 1 | Most international carriers, along with domestic and foreign airlines | Built earlier and the largest; Metro Line 5 has a station here |
| T2 | Terminal 2 | Aeroméxico (hub) and SkyTeam | Newer, with long-haul and connecting flights concentrated here |

**How to tell where you are:**

- **The Aeroméxico group is in T2**; almost all other carriers are in T1 — start with the airline, then check your boarding pass.
- T1 ↔ T2 has two links: the **airside Aerotrén monorail, which is free** (boarding pass required, roughly 05:00–23:00), or the **landside shuttle bus, free or low-cost** (24 hours).
- For a cross-terminal connection **allow 45–60 minutes**.

## Transfers and connections

1. A connection inside T1 or inside T2 is quickest; for a cross-terminal connection allow more than 1.5 hours.
2. The airside cross-terminal link depends on the Aerotrén and on your boarding pass, and **it stops running late at night**, leaving only the landside shuttle.
3. Mexico City sits at about 2,200 metres, and a long-haul flight takes more out of you here, so err on the generous side with connection time.

## Routes and flights

- **Routes** — around 20-plus airlines flying to about 100 destinations; long-haul points include Madrid, Barcelona, Paris, Amsterdam, London, Tokyo, São Paulo and Buenos Aires.
- **Flights** — 44.61 million passengers in 2025 (27.12 million domestic, 17.48 million international), down about 1.7% as some services have shifted to NLU.
- **Aircraft** — main base is **Aeroméxico (T2) and SkyTeam**, followed by Viva Aerobús and Volaris; the fleet is 737 and A320 family narrowbodies, with Aeroméxico using 787 and 777 on long-haul.

## Getting into Mexico City

- **Metro Line 5** — has a station on the T1 side and costs very little; the main way to dodge the traffic, but **T2 has no direct metro**, so you must shuttle to T1 first.
- **The Metro Line 1 extension** — connects parts of the network to the area around the airport.
- **Airport buses** — cover the main hubs and hotel districts of the city.
- **Taxi / rideshare** — authorised taxis must be paid for at a counter inside the terminal; rideshare pick-ups are from set points.

## Practical tips

- **The biggest trap is confusing the airports**: Mexico City now has **Benito Juárez (MEX) and Felipe Ángeles (NLU)**, with NLU 35–50 km north of the city and used mainly by low-cost carriers. **Check the three-letter code before booking or arranging a pick-up.**
- Travellers heading for NLU can take the **suburban railway that opened in April 2026** (direct from Buenavista station in the north of the city) instead of relying on a long coach ride.
- Traffic in Mexico City is severe, so **when you have a flight to catch, budget for peak road conditions** — rail is the only predictable option.
